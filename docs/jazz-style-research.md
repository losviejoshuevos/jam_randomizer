# Jazz style research

## Scope

This is a compositional study, not a ranking. The twenty recordings were selected as canonical, contrasting models for a playable Jam Randomizer profile: swing, songbook harmony, bebop, hard bop, jazz blues, modal/post-bop, jazz waltz and fusion. Full copyrighted forms are not copied; the table records structural traits that can be transformed into original patterns.

## Twenty reference recordings

| # | Recording | Direction | Form / harmonic observation | Generator lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Duke Ellington Orchestra — “Take the ‘A’ Train” | Swing | 32-bar AABA; the A phrase establishes I, brightens with II7 and returns through ii–V; the bridge moves to IV before preparing home. | Medium harmonic rhythm, strong A identity, one secondary dominant, functional bridge. |
| 2 | Cannonball Adderley — “Autumn Leaves” | Songbook / hard bop reading | Relative major and minor are linked by descending-fifth ii–V–I and ii half-diminished–V–i motion. | Smooth cycle-five voice leading is more important than chord count. |
| 3 | Thelonious Monk — “‘Round Midnight” | Jazz ballad | Minor AABA with chromatic inner lines, altered dominants and dark delayed cadences. | Slow changes can carry richer color; endings may delay tonic without losing it. |
| 4 | Charlie Parker — “Anthropology” | Bebop / rhythm changes | AABA contrafact model: tonic turnarounds in A, cycle dominants in the bridge, rapid target-oriented rhythm. | Bebop density needs explicit destinations and compact half-bar events. |
| 5 | Charlie Parker — “Blues for Alice” | Bebop / Bird blues | Twelve-bar blues landmarks are preserved beneath descending ii–V connections and rapid tonicizations. | Advanced blues can be chromatic while I, IV and turnaround positions remain audible. |
| 6 | Dizzy Gillespie — “A Night in Tunisia” | Bebop / Afro-Cuban | Minor-centered ostinato and chromatic dominant color contrast with a more functional release. | A static hook plus active release is a strong A/B design. |
| 7 | John Coltrane — “Giant Steps” | Multi-tonic post-bop | Major-third-related tonal centers are connected by compressed dominant–tonic arrivals. | Multi-tonic symmetry belongs only to advanced/adventurous material and must stay rare. |
| 8 | Art Blakey & the Jazz Messengers — “Moanin’” | Hard bop | Blues/gospel call-and-response riff is completed by a more functional bridge. | A memorable vamp can define A; B must provide release rather than a suffix-only variation. |
| 9 | Horace Silver — “Song for My Father” | Hard bop / soul jazz | Minor Latin groove and descending-color vamp contrast with ii–V motion in the release. | Groove, rests and bass identity can outweigh harmonic complexity. |
| 10 | Miles Davis — “So What” | Modal | 32-bar AABA: long Dorian tonic region, bridge transposed up a semitone, then return. | Modal contrast can be a new center with the same quality; no routine V–I is required. |
| 11 | John Coltrane — “Impressions” | Up-tempo modal | The “So What” modal plan is energized by faster tempo and a persistent rhythmic surface. | Tempo and rhythm can radically change an identical sparse harmonic design. |
| 12 | Herbie Hancock — “Maiden Voyage” | Modal / post-bop | AABA made from long sus/quartal fields; four-measure colors replace functional cadences. | Use sus/modal centers as complete sonorities and move them sparingly. |
| 13 | Wayne Shorter — “Footprints” | Post-bop / modal blues | A twelve-bar minor-blues outline in 6/4 is obscured by modal bass motion and an unconventional turnaround. | A familiar form can survive altered meter/turnaround; current MVP adapts the idea to 3/4 or 4/4. |
| 14 | Dave Brubeck Quartet — “Take Five” | Cool jazz / odd meter | Two-chord minor vamp in 5/4 with a contrasting diatonic bridge. | The MVP cannot emit 5/4 yet, but should preserve the vamp-versus-release architecture. |
| 15 | Bill Evans — “Waltz for Debby” | Jazz waltz | 3/4 lyrical form with tonic prolongation, cycle motion and subtle functional arrivals. | Jazz 3/4 needs full-bar rhythm and spacious changes, not half-bar splitting. |
| 16 | Stan Getz & João Gilberto — “The Girl from Ipanema” | Bossa jazz | Long tonic color, II7/Lydian-dominant brightness and a bridge moving through less stable tonal regions. | Bossa needs gentle voice leading and restrained density rather than bebop speed. |
| 17 | Herbie Hancock — “Chameleon” | Jazz-funk fusion | Extended i–IV Dorian vamp; later contrasting vamp and layered timbral development. | Fusion A can be two chords for many bars; development can change center or sonority, not cadence. |
| 18 | Chick Corea / Return to Forever — “Spain” | Latin fusion | Flamenco-derived introduction and a cyclical solo progression with major-seventh arrivals and strong dominants. | Fusion may combine a memorable modal/Latin hook with a clearly navigable functional cycle. |
| 19 | Miles Davis — “Shhh/Peaceful” from *In a Silent Way* | Early fusion | Open D Mixolydian field, bass drone and straight-eighth electric groove. | Open-form fusion should prioritize pedal, texture and duration over chord churn. |
| 20 | Weather Report — “Birdland” | Fusion | Multiple riff-led sections, register changes and groove-based harmonic blocks create development. | Fusion form can be assembled from distinct hooks whose orchestration/rhythm carries continuity. |

## Resulting internal archetypes

1. `swing-standard`: functional A, selective II7/secondary dominant, IV or relative-key bridge.
2. `bebop-cycle`: compressed ii–V chains, turnarounds and rare substitute dominant.
3. `hard-bop-gospel`: riff/vamp A with blues or gospel identity and a functional release.
4. `bird-blues`: jazz-blues landmarks with descending connections and a clearer turnaround.
5. `jazz-ballad`: minor or major slow harmony, line-cliché feeling, borrowed minor subdominant and delayed cadence.
6. `modal-quartal`: long Dorian/Mixolydian/sus regions and section-level transposition/color change.
7. `jazz-waltz`: spacious 3/4-compatible functional motion without half-bar events.
8. `fusion-open`: Dorian/Mixolydian groove, sus/quartal color, occasional constant-structure or functional release.

## Generator constraints derived from the study

- Harmonic freedom controls roots, tonicizations and substitutions. Chord complexity controls extensions and alterations.
- Easy keeps plain, readable chord symbols even when the structural pattern comes from jazz.
- Medium introduces sixth/seventh/ninth and sus vocabulary.
- Advanced widens the palette to 11/13, maj7#11, altered dominants, half-diminished and diminished approaches, while retaining simpler chords.
- Chromatic devices must have targets: secondary dominant → target a fifth below; tritone substitute → target a semitone below; diminished approach → adjacent target.
- A session receives at most a small number of high-impact chromatic devices. Advanced does not mean every chord is altered.
- Modal and fusion archetypes explicitly avoid automatic ii–V–I filling.
- Themes B–D must differ structurally from earlier themes, not merely by chord suffix.
- No fractional-bar events in 3/4. In 4/4, half-bar changes are concentrated in bebop and Bird-blues patterns.
